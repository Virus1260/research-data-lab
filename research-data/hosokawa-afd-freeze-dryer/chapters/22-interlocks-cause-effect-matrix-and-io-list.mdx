# 22 — Interlocks: Cause & Effect Matrix and I/O List

See `pid_controls_data/cause_and_effect_matrix.csv` (13 interlocks) and `pid_controls_data/io_list.csv` (30 tags) for the full structured data behind this file.

## 1. What a Cause & Effect (C&E) Matrix actually is, and why it's the right format

A C&E matrix is the standard way the process industry documents interlock logic — standardized in **IEC 62881**. The idea is deliberately simple: **rows are causes** (a condition becomes true), **columns are effects** (an action the system takes in response), and a mark at the intersection means "this cause triggers this effect." The entire value of the format is that it's unambiguous and exhaustively checkable — an auditor, a commissioning engineer, or you in six months can look at any row and know exactly what happens, with no logic buried in PLC code that only makes sense if you already know what it does.

## 2. Reading the columns in `cause_and_effect_matrix.csv`

- **System** — whether this interlock lives in the BPCS or the SIS (file 20). Notice most of the genuinely safety-critical rows (E-stop, lid/pressure permissives, oxygen deficiency) are SIS; rows that protect equipment or product quality rather than people (agitator jam, filter clogging, vacuum pump feedback loss) are BPCS-only. This split is itself a design decision worth making deliberately, row by row, rather than defaulting everything to one system.
- **Voting Logic** — how many independent sensors must agree before the action fires. `1oo1` ("one out of one") means a single sensor's reading is trusted directly — simplest, but that one sensor is a single point of failure. `1oo2` means either of two independent sensors can trigger the action — biased toward safety (a single failed sensor reading "trip" still trips the system, so you never miss a real hazard, at the cost of occasional nuisance trips from a single faulty sensor). `2oo3` (not needed anywhere on this machine at this scale, but common on larger plants) means two of three must agree — this reduces nuisance trips while still tolerating one failed sensor, at the cost of needing three sensors instead of one.
- **Illustrative SIL** — Safety Integrity Level, the IEC 61511 measure of how reliable a safety function needs to be (SIL 1 through SIL 4, increasing reliability requirement). **These are illustrative placeholders, not a certified result.** The only correct way to assign a real SIL to a real interlock is a **LOPA (Layer of Protection Analysis)** — a formal, documented risk assessment specific to your actual product, your actual facility, and the actual consequence of each specific failure, carried out by (or with) a qualified process-safety engineer. Treat the SIL column here as "roughly this order of importance," useful for prioritizing your build effort, and nothing more until a real LOPA has been done.

## 3. Two interlocks worth understanding in detail (because the *reasoning*, not just the row, is the transferable skill)

- **I-04 and I-13 both pair an electronic BPCS/SIS trip with a passive mechanical device (`VRV-101`, `PSV-501`).** This is intentional layering, not redundancy for its own sake: a passive relief device needs no power, no logic, and no sensor to work — it protects you even if the entire control system (BPCS *and* SIS) has lost power or failed outright. This is exactly the kind of "independent layer of protection" thinking a real LOPA formalizes, and it's a good habit to build into your own designs before you ever run a formal LOPA: **for the handful of consequences you genuinely cannot tolerate, ask whether a passive device can protect you even in a total-control-system-failure scenario, and if so, include one.**
- **I-08 (seal barrier gas pressure) only applies if you choose the double-mechanical-seal agitator drive option from file 05's addendum**, rather than the magnetic coupling or single-seal alternatives. This is a good small example of why the interlock list isn't fixed in stone — it's a direct consequence of specific design choices you make elsewhere in the machine (file 05, file 06), and should be revisited every time one of those choices changes.

## 4. Reading the I/O list

`io_list.csv` is the flat version of every tag on the P&ID (file 19) plus the two SIS-only tags this file adds (`I-08`'s `PIT-701`, and `ESD-001`/`AT-601` which don't appear as circles on the P&ID itself since they're SIS field devices drawn separately in the control architecture, file 20). Columns: **Tag**, **Description**, **System** (BPCS/SIS), **Signal Type** (the actual electrical/data type — analog 4-20mA, discrete on/off, RTD, etc.), **Range/Notes**, and **P&ID Ref** (where it physically sits). This is the document you'd actually hand to whoever configures PLC I/O modules, and it's also useful as a cross-check list against your SolidWorks model once you get there (file 24) — every tag here needs a corresponding physical fitting, boss, or nozzle somewhere on the 3D model.

## 5. What this file deliberately does not do

It does not certify any interlock to any SIL level, and it does not replace a real Dust Hazard Analysis (file 09 §4) or LOPA. What it gives you is the **complete, structured, auditable list of what this specific machine needs to protect against and how**, in the standard industry format, ready to be reviewed, revised, and formally validated by qualified people as your build moves from prototype toward anything GMP-capable (file 14).
