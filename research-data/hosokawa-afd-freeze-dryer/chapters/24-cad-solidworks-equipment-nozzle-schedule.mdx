# 24 — CAD/SolidWorks Preparation: Equipment, Nozzle & Valve Schedule

See `pid_controls_data/cad_equipment_nozzle_schedule.json` for the full structured schedule this file explains. This is written specifically for the moment you sit down in SolidWorks: it's the list of *what needs a nozzle, what standard that nozzle follows, and what rating the surrounding shell needs to be modeled to* — the information a P&ID alone doesn't give you, because a P&ID deliberately shows process connectivity, not physical geometry.

## 1. Why a separate schedule from the P&ID

The P&ID (file 19) tells you *that* the vessel has a vapor outlet to the material collector. It doesn't tell you *what size that nozzle is, what flange or clamp standard it uses, or what gasket material to specify*. That's exactly the gap this file and its JSON close — it's the traditional handoff document between a process engineer and a mechanical/CAD engineer on any real project, and there's no reason to skip it just because it's currently a one-person project.

## 2. Reading the equipment schedule

For each major tag, the schedule gives: a capacity/volume basis, **design pressure on both the process side and the jacket side separately** (these are genuinely different numbers — the process side lives mostly under vacuum with a modest positive-pressure allowance, while a half-pipe-coil jacket needs a materially higher rating to handle real circulation pressure, file 05's addendum), a design temperature range with margin above your actual operating range (this margin matters for material toughness/MDMT checks per file 05 §5, not just as a round-number buffer), and materials of construction split between product-contact and non-product-contact surfaces (file 05 §4, file 10). Package equipment (`PKG-301`, `PKG-401`, `TCU-201`, etc.) is deliberately left light on internal detail — you don't design a vendor's vacuum pump train, you model its purchased envelope and nozzle-connection drawing once you've selected a vendor.

## 3. Reading the nozzle schedule

Thirteen nozzles are listed against the vessel (`V-101`) — this is a realistic, complete count for this kind of machine, not a partial list. A few worth flagging:

- **N2 (vapor outlet) deliberately doesn't get a fixed size** — it's sized by a real vacuum-conductance calculation (file 08 §6's note that vacuum lines want to be short and large-diameter), not by convention. Undersizing this one nozzle is one of the easier ways to quietly bottleneck your whole vacuum system's real-world pumping speed regardless of how well the pump itself is specified.
- **N5 (agitator shaft penetration) has no standard DN size at all** — because it isn't a process nozzle in the usual sense; its geometry is dictated entirely by whichever magnetic-coupling or mechanical-seal assembly you purchase (file 05 §3 and its addendum). Get this vendor's mounting-flange drawing *before* finalizing the lid geometry in CAD, not after — it's one of the few components on this machine where the purchased part's geometry should drive your design rather than the other way around.
- **N12 (bottom discharge) is deliberately shown with two possible connection philosophies**: a straightforward sanitary Tri-Clamp for a first prototype, or an aseptic split-valve docking connection (the kind of device that lets a canister or vial-filler connect and disconnect without ever exposing the powder to room air) if you're building toward the fully aseptic operation Hosokawa's own literature describes for the real AFD (file 18, source 21). Decide which philosophy you're building toward *before* modeling this nozzle in detail, since the two approaches have quite different envelope and clearance requirements underneath the vessel.
- **Face-to-face dimensions are deliberately not invented for the Tri-Clamp/ISO-K fittings.** Sanitary clamp fittings don't really have a single "face-to-face" dimension the way a flanged pipe spool does — their geometry is fully defined by the ISO 2852/DIN 32676 (Tri-Clamp) or ISO 2861 series (ISO-K/ISO-KF vacuum flange) standards for whatever nominal size you choose. Pull the real numbers from those standards (or your fitting vendor's own catalog drawing, which will cite the same standard) when you model each nozzle — that's a five-minute catalog lookup once you've chosen sizes, and it will be more accurate than any number this package could give you generically.

## 4. Reading the valve schedule

The one column worth thinking about carefully, valve by valve, is **fail position** — what a valve does if it loses air supply or electrical signal, which is a genuine safety design decision, not a default setting:
- Most valves here fail **closed (FC)** — losing power/air should not, by itself, create a new hazardous condition (e.g., `FCV-201` failing closed simply stops jacket flow, which is safe).
- `PV-102` (the vessel-to-collector vacuum path) is flagged as needing real thought: failing open toward atmosphere would break vacuum uncontrolled mid-cycle, which is its own kind of process upset — so fail-closed or fail-in-last-position is generally preferable, but confirm this against your own safe-state analysis (file 09, file 22) rather than copying this schedule blindly.
- `XV-104` (the vent/vacuum-break valve) is the one genuinely debatable case: some designs deliberately choose **fail-open** here specifically *so that* a total power/air loss automatically breaks vacuum and returns the vessel toward a safe, atmospheric, accessible state — this is a legitimate alternative safety philosophy to "everything fails closed," and which one is right depends on which failure mode you've judged more dangerous for your specific installation (an uncontrolled vacuum break vs. a vessel stuck under vacuum with no power). This is exactly the kind of decision a real HAZOP/LOPA exercise (file 09 §6, file 22 §2) is meant to resolve deliberately rather than by default.

## 5. What to do with this in SolidWorks, practically

A reasonable modeling sequence: block in the vessel shell and jacket geometry first (file 05, sized via file 11) with placeholder nozzle bosses at the thirteen locations above; source real vendor drawings for the purchased items that dictate their own interface geometry (the agitator seal/coupling, the discharge valve, the sight glass, the vacuum pump train's inlet flange) and drop those in as the true geometric constraints they are; then finalize nozzle sizes, orientations, and standard-compliant dimensions once those vendor interfaces are locked, rather than the other way around. This avoids the common and frustrating rework of having designed a beautiful vessel around a nozzle size or orientation that turns out not to match the seal or valve you actually end up buying.
