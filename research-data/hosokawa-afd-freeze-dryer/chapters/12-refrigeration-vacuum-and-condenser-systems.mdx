# 12 — Refrigeration, Cold-Trap Condenser & Vacuum Skids

## 1. The Temperature Control Unit (TCU)

Hosokawa names the TCU explicitly as a standalone subsystem on the AFD page ("Temperature Control Unit (TCU) for thermal management"). Generically, a TCU is a self-contained skid that:
- Houses a **refrigeration circuit** (compressor, condenser, expansion device, evaporator) sized to chill a heat-transfer fluid.
- Houses **electric heating elements** to warm the same fluid loop for the drying phases.
- Circulates that fluid through the process vessel's jacket via a pump, with a control loop (PID, typically) holding jacket temperature to setpoint and ramping it through the cycle's freeze/dry stages.
- **Heat-transfer fluid**: for the wide temperature swing a freeze dryer needs (roughly −55 °C to +40/50 °C or higher for a demanding secondary-drying step), **silicone (dimethyl siloxane) heat-transfer oil** is the industry-standard fluid, used because it stays fluid and stable across that entire range without freezing or excessive viscosity increase at the cold end — this is standard practice on conventional shelf freeze dryers as well and transfers directly.

## 2. Refrigeration system design

- **Cascade refrigeration** — two (or more) separate refrigerant loops connected through an intermediate heat exchanger, where the "low stage" loop's condenser is cooled by the "high stage" loop's evaporator — is the standard architecture whenever a single refrigerant/compressor combination can't efficiently reach the target low temperature (roughly below about −40 °C, where a single-stage vapor-compression cycle's efficiency collapses and compressor discharge temperatures become excessive). This is standard practice on virtually all pharma/lab freeze dryers reaching the −40 °C to −85 °C range typically needed.
- **Natural refrigerants** are increasingly used for the low-temperature stage because of regulatory pressure (Montreal/Kyoto Protocol phase-downs of high-GWP synthetic refrigerants): **ethane** (boiling point −88.5 °C) is specifically cited by GEA as enabling condenser temperatures below −80 °C in an indirect, silicone-oil-cooled arrangement — a genuinely low-global-warming-potential option worth considering for a from-scratch build, alongside more conventional (but higher-GWP) choices like R404A or R507.
- **Direct expansion (DX)** cooling — refrigerant routed straight through a coil in the vessel jacket or condenser — is simpler but uncontrolled: run continuously, it just "bottoms out" at whatever temperature the refrigeration circuit's coldest point reaches, with no fine temperature control. Most pharma-grade systems instead use **indirect cooling**: refrigerant chills the heat-transfer fluid via a heat exchanger, and that fluid (not refrigerant) circulates through the jacket, giving much tighter, controllable temperature regulation (typically ±1 °C on commercial shelf-dryer literature) and simpler sealing (no refrigerant lines running to a moving/agitated vessel).
- **Shared vs. separate refrigeration for the vessel jacket and the condenser**: advanced commercial freeze dryers often use one shared refrigeration plant for both duties (fewer components, better reliability, better energy efficiency, less waste heat) rather than two independent systems — worth designing in from the start rather than retrofitting.

## 3. Vacuum system

### 3a. Pump selection

![Industrial Pharmaceutical Vacuum Pump Skid — Multi-Stage Dry Screw, Roots Booster & Condenser Module](/photos/vacuum_pump_skid_pharma.webp)

A typical pharma freeze-dryer vacuum train is **oil-free / dry**, both to avoid oil vapor backstreaming contaminating the sterile product path and because condensable water vapor is hard on oil-sealed pumps. Common architecture, per general vacuum-industry and freeze-drying-specific literature (Edwards Vacuum's own freeze-drying application note):
- A **positive-displacement roots blower** (or dry screw pump) staged ahead of a **backing pump**, in series, is a very common combination — matches the two-(or-more)-stage vacuum pump system Hosokawa's own 2003 patent specifically recommends for a 50 L charge.
- Dry screw pumps and multi-stage Roots/backing-pump combinations are the modern default for pharma freeze-drying, replacing older oil-sealed rotary vane pumps for product-contact-adjacent duty.
- **The vacuum pump pulls through the condenser/material collector, not directly on the product chamber** — this protects the pump from ingesting bulk water vapor and any entrained product dust (both patents and generic literature agree on this arrangement).

### 3b. Pressure instrumentation — and why you need two kinds of gauge
This is one of the more subtle, easy-to-get-wrong parts of the system, and it's worth understanding in some depth because it directly enables in-process end-of-drying detection:
- **Pirani gauge (`PIT-101B`)**: Measures pressure indirectly via the thermal conductivity of the gas present. Its reading is **strongly gas-composition dependent** — water vapor has a thermal conductivity approximately **1.6× higher than dry nitrogen**. Consequently, while the chamber atmosphere is saturated with sublimed water vapor (active primary drying), the Pirani reads significantly higher than true pressure:
  $$\text{Ratio} = \frac{P_{Pirani}}{P_{Capacitance\,Manometer}} \approx 1.55 - 1.70$$
- **Capacitance manometer (`PIT-101A`, e.g., MKS Baratron)**: Measures true absolute pressure via mechanical deflection of an Inconel diaphragm, completely independent of gas species or composition.
- **Comparative pressure measurement & Automated Phase Advance**: Running both gauges on the vessel chamber and watching the ratio converge toward unity ($1.0$) is the industry gold standard for detecting the **end of primary drying**:
  $$\lim_{t \to t_{end}} \left(\frac{P_{Pirani}}{P_{CM}}\right) = 1.00 \pm 0.05 \quad \iff \quad \Delta P = P_{Pirani} - P_{CM} < 0.010\text{ mbar}$$
  When the gap closes below $0.010\text{ mbar}$ for $\ge 15$ continuous minutes, sublimed water vapor evolution has ceased, and the PLC automatically triggers the recipe advance into Secondary Drying (desorption).
- Control loops (`PIC-101`) must always regulate chamber pressure using the **capacitance manometer** (true physical pressure), with the Pirani gauge serving as a diagnostic and phase-advance sensor.

### 3c. Achievable vacuum level
Per file 02/03, the process needs roughly **5 mbar down to 0.01 mbar** (Hosokawa's 2003 patent range) or the narrower **0.1–3 mbar** figure given in the 2020 patent for approaching the sublimation onset — comfortably within the range of a well-selected dry screw + Roots combination, and well short of the deep high-vacuum range (10⁻³ mbar and below) that would require diffusion or turbomolecular pumping. This is a "rough/medium vacuum" system, not a high-vacuum one — an important, cost-relevant distinction when selecting pumps.

### 3d. Vacuum Leak Rate Specification & Qualification (ISO 13408-3)
Aseptic pharmaceutical lyophilization mandates rigorous chamber leak-tightness verification to prevent microbial ingress and ensure process repeatability. Per **ISO 13408-3** (*Aseptic processing of health care products — Part 3: Lyophilization*), the maximum permissible gas ingress rate for an isolated, clean, empty freeze-drying vessel is:

$$Q_{leak} = \frac{V_{system} \cdot \Delta P}{\Delta t} \le 0.010\text{ mbar}\cdot\text{L/s} \quad (10\ \mu\text{bar}\cdot\text{L/s})$$

Where:
- $V_{system}$ = Total internal volume of the conical vessel, dome, and isolated vacuum lines ($\text{L}$).
- $\Delta P$ = Pressure rise observed over the hold test ($\text{mbar}$).
- $\Delta t$ = Test duration, typically standard 30-minute ($1,800\text{ s}$) hold after initial pump-down below $0.02\text{ mbar}$.

In the AFD recipe sequence (Chapter 21), an automated 10-minute leak-check phase runs before every product charging operation. If $Q_{leak} > 0.015\text{ mbar}\cdot\text{L/s}$, the batch sequence interlocks and prevents product loading.

## 4. Condenser vs. Heated Vapor Filter Dome (Material Collector)

In an agitated vacuum freeze dryer, the vapor extraction path must handle both high-velocity sublimed vapor and fine powder fines:

![Classic Nauta Jacketed Vacuum Dryer Internal Filter Cutaway](/photos/classic_nauta_vacuum_dryer_internal_filter_cutaway.jpg)

### A. The Heated Vapor Filter Dome
- **Anti-Condensation Heating Jacket**: The filter dome housing mounted directly atop the conical vessel must be jacketed and heated (typically +35 °C to +50 °C). If the dome were unheated, sublimed vapor would encounter cold metal, condense as liquid droplets, and turn dry powder fines into a gummy paste that irreversibly blinds the filter elements.
- **Filter Media**: Sintered stainless steel porous mesh or PTFE membrane cartridges rated for sub-micron powder retention ($0.2 - 1.0\ \mu\text{m}$).
- **Automatic Reverse-Pulse Nitrogen Blowback**: Periodic pressurized nitrogen pulses (4–6 bar g, 50–100 ms duration) backflush each filter element sequentially. Dislodged dry powder drops straight back into the agitated conical bed, ensuring 100% batch yield without manual recovery.

### B. Cryogenic Cold Trap Condenser vs. Liquid Condenser
A critical engineering distinction separates thermal vacuum drying from vacuum freeze drying:

| Operating System | Chamber Pressure Range | Vapor Stream Characteristics | Condenser Configuration & Temperature | Effluent / Phase Collected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Thermal Vacuum Dryer** *(e.g. Nauta Evaporator)* | $20\text{ to }100\text{ mbar abs}$ | Dense vapor flow through compact DN50–DN80 piping | Shell & Tube Condenser cooled by chilled water ($+15^\circ\text{C}$ to $-10^\circ\text{C}$) | Continuous liquid solvent drain to receiver drum |
| **Vacuum Freeze Dryer** *(Hosokawa AFD)* | $0.05\text{ to }0.5\text{ mbar abs}$ | Low-density sublimed vapor through oversized DN200–DN300 duct | Cryogenic Cold Trap coils ($-55^\circ\text{C}$ to $-85^\circ\text{C}$) | Solid ice frost layer frozen directly on coils (zero batch drain) |

- **Why Liquid Condensation is Thermodynamically Impossible**: Under active freeze-drying conditions ($P_{ch} \approx 0.1\text{ mbar}$), water vapor cannot exist in the liquid state. The condenser does **not** produce liquid drainage during the drying cycle; incoming water vapor desublimates directly as **solid ice frost** onto cryogenic cooling coils maintained at **$-55 ^\circ\text{C}$ to $-85 ^\circ\text{C}$**.
- **Condenser Ice Frost Thermal Resistance Model**: As primary drying proceeds, ice accumulates on the coil surfaces. Frost has an effective thermal conductivity of $k_{ice} \approx 2.2\text{ W/m}\cdot\text{K}$. As the ice layer thickness ($\delta_{ice}$) grows, the temperature of the ice outer surface ($T_{ice,surf}$) rises above the refrigerant coil temperature ($T_{coil}$):
  $$\Delta T_{ice} = T_{ice,surf} - T_{coil} = \frac{\dot{q}_{sub} \cdot \delta_{ice}}{k_{ice} \cdot A_{cond}}$$
  Where $\dot{q}_{sub} = \dot{m}_{sub} \cdot \Delta H_{sub}$. If $\delta_{ice}$ exceeds **$12 - 15\text{ mm}$**, the surface temperature of the ice cake can rise above $-35^\circ\text{C}$, elevating the saturation vapor pressure at the condenser and choking the sublimation driving force ($\Delta P = P_{ch} - P_{ice,cond}$).
- **Coil Geometry**: Condenser cooling coils must be engineered with a generous **inter-tube spacing of at least 35–45 mm** to allow up to $12\text{ mm}$ of ice frost growth without bridging between adjacent tubes.
- **Defrosting Phase**: Once the batch finishes and the chamber is isolated, the condenser is brought to atmospheric pressure and defrosted via circulating hot water, steam injection, or reverse hot-gas refrigerant bypass. Only during this post-cycle phase does water drain into a condensate receiver.

## 5. Valve requirements on the vacuum path

- **Isolation valve** between vessel and material collector/condenser — must be vacuum-rated, and per NL2026893B1, ideally able to hold an intermediate ("throttled") position as well as fully open/closed, since the patent's whole recirculation strategy depends on that valve's position changing through the cycle.
- **Bypass line and valve** around the main isolation valve, per the same patent.
- **Vent/break valve** to controllably return the vessel to atmospheric pressure (through a sterile air or nitrogen filter, for aseptic operation) at end of cycle.
- All vacuum-side valves need bellows-sealed or diaphragm-sealed stems (not simple packed-gland stems) to avoid becoming the system's dominant leak path — standard vacuum-engineering practice, not AFD-specific.
