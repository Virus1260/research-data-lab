# 23 — Engineering Resolution: How the Freezing Stage Actually Works

This file answers three questions that trip up almost everyone new to this equipment: *can you just dump dry ice or liquid nitrogen straight into the product?*, *how does the jacket actually get cold and then hot again?*, and *how do you size the thermal duty for the freezing stage?* These are good questions to have gotten stuck on — they're exactly where the real engineering judgment in this machine lives.

## 1. Direct product-contact freezing: what the patent allows vs. what's actually wise

**What Hosokawa's own 2003 patent (NL1022668C2, file 03) discloses:** two freezing methods are explicitly claimed as viable — (a) indirect cooling via the jacket, and (b) direct injection of liquid nitrogen or solid CO₂ straight into the vessel, mixed with the charge. This is a documented fact about what the patent covers, not something to walk back.

**Why direct injection is nonetheless the wrong default choice for most real pharmaceutical products**, and worth treating as a specialist option rather than a shortcut:

- **Carbon dioxide directly acidifies aqueous product.** CO₂ dissolves into water to form carbonic acid (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻). This is the same chemistry that makes carbonated water mildly acidic (typically pH ~3.5–4 at saturation) — and it will do the same thing to an aqueous drug solution exposed to CO₂ gas or solid CO₂. For anything pH-sensitive — most therapeutic proteins, many small-molecule APIs with pH-dependent stability, essentially any emulsion or cream formulation — an uncontrolled pH excursion into the 3.5–4.5 range is a real degradation/precipitation risk, not a theoretical one. This concern does not apply equally to every product (a robust small-molecule solution with a wide stability pH window may genuinely tolerate it), but it must be evaluated formulation-by-formulation, not assumed away.
- **Vacuum system loading is a real, calculable problem.** Solid CO₂ sublimes into a very large gas volume, and that effect gets dramatically larger at process vacuum rather than smaller. Using the ideal gas law (`PV = nRT`) to compare 1 kg of CO₂ (≈22.7 mol) at standard conditions vs. at a typical primary-drying pressure of roughly 0.04 mbar (4 Pa):
  - At STP (1 atm, 0°C): V ≈ n × 22.4 L/mol ≈ **510 L**.
  - At 0.04 mbar and roughly 0°C (273 K): V = nRT/P ≈ (22.7 mol × 8.314 J/mol·K × 273 K) / 4 Pa ≈ **13 million liters**.
  This is the correct order of magnitude, and it's a genuinely useful thing to have checked yourself rather than taken on faith: introducing bulk solid CO₂ into the vessel at process vacuum means your vacuum pump train — which file 06 sizes for the *water vapor* sublimation load — would suddenly need to swallow a gas volume many orders of magnitude larger, for as long as that CO₂ keeps subliming. A pump train sized correctly for water vapor removal will not handle this without being dramatically oversized for that one purpose alone, or without a large, transient pressure excursion that disrupts the whole process.
  - **Liquid nitrogen has a related but distinct problem: rapid volumetric expansion on vaporization.** LN₂ expands roughly 1:700 (1 L of liquid becomes ~700 L of gas) as it warms to ambient temperature. Dumped in bulk into an agitated vessel, this expansion happens fast enough to behave like a small gas explosion inside the chamber (a version of the Leidenfrost effect — a vapor film forms around the still-liquid nitrogen, insulating it and delaying full vaporization until it happens all at once) — capable of blowing fine powder/product into the vacuum ductwork and, in a closed vessel, producing a genuine overpressure event.
- **Industrial dry ice and LN₂ are not pharmaceutical-grade materials by default.** Commodity dry ice and bulk LN₂ dewars are produced and handled to industrial gas standards, not to pharmaceutical raw-material standards (no certificate of analysis for bioburden, endotoxin, or particulate content as supplied). Using either in direct product contact on a GMP line requires sourcing a pharmaceutical-grade, appropriately certified cryogen — a real, and often underestimated, supply-chain and qualification requirement, not just a purchasing preference.

**The practical conclusion:** treat indirect jacket cooling as the correct default for essentially any real pharmaceutical formulation, exactly as this package's earlier files already assume throughout. Direct cryogen injection remains a legitimate, patent-disclosed option worth knowing about — it may suit robust, non-pH-sensitive products where the vacuum-loading and sourcing issues have been specifically engineered around — but it is a specialist choice requiring its own dedicated qualification work, not a simpler alternative to jacket cooling.

## 2. How the jacket actually gets cold, then hot again — the TCU/HTF architecture in depth

Building on file 06 §1, here is the mechanism in more detail:

- **A single heat-transfer fluid (HTF) loop serves both the freezing and drying stages.** The same fluid that's chilled to around −55 °C for freezing is, later in the same batch, heated to drive primary and secondary drying, and potentially heated further still for a post-SIP thermal bake-out. This only works because the fluid itself stays liquid and pumpable across that entire span. **Dow's Syltherm™ XLT** is a real, commercially available, pharma-industry-used example of such a fluid — a silicone (polydimethylsiloxane) heat-transfer fluid with a usable liquid range of about −100 °C to +260 °C and a freeze point below −111 °C, explicitly marketed for single-fluid heating-and-cooling batch process systems in pharmaceutical and fine-chemical service. It is one legitimate, named example of the "wide-range silicone heat-transfer fluid" this package has referred to generically in files 06 and 11 — not a confirmed statement of what Hosokawa itself uses, which isn't published.
- **Cooling the loop**: the HTF is cooled by rejecting its heat into a colder refrigeration circuit through a heat exchanger (a brazed-plate or shell-and-tube design is standard) — this is the cascade refrigeration system described in file 06 §2. A facility with access to a bulk LN₂ supply can alternatively (or additionally) use a cryogenic heat exchanger metered by a proportional control valve as the cold source for the HTF loop, rather than (or alongside) a mechanical cascade compressor system — this is a legitimate design alternative worth costing against a full cascade refrigeration plant, particularly for a lower-duty prototype where bottled/bulk LN₂ may be simpler to procure than commissioning a full refrigeration skid.
- **Heating the loop**: a simple **inline electric heater** in the HTF circulation path (on the order of 15–25 kW for a small-to-pilot-scale vessel — the correct number depends entirely on your actual heat-transfer-area and ramp-rate targets from file 11's method, not a fixed constant) raises the same fluid's temperature for the drying stages. Because it's the same fluid and the same circulation loop throughout, there's no fluid-changeover step and no risk of cross-contaminating a "hot" and "cold" circuit — a real practical advantage of the single-fluid approach over, say, separately piped brine and steam systems.
- **The jacket itself is not under vacuum** — only the product side of the vessel wall is. The jacket is a conventional (if wide-temperature-range) liquid-filled circulation space; file 05's addendum on jacket construction (plain annular, half-pipe coil, or dimple) describes the real options for how that space is physically built.
- **Insulation** sits on the *outside* of the jacket, not inside it — a high-performance insulation (rigid closed-cell foam for a mid-range design, or a cryogenic-grade aerogel/cellular-glass insulation if the coldest end of the range and minimizing radial thickness both matter) wrapped in a removable, cleanable stainless cladding, consistent with file 07 §4's general guidance.

## 3. How agitation, jacket temperature, and vacuum together produce granular freezing (not a solid block)

This is the mechanism worth being able to explain in one breath, because it's the core trick the whole machine is built around: **continuous slow agitation (a gentle wall-scraping action — think single-digit to low-teens RPM, not a high-shear mixer, per file 05 §3's note that only gentle mixing is needed) keeps breaking up the product against the cold jacket wall as it freezes**, so instead of the whole batch solidifying into one block (which would then be nearly impossible to dry uniformly), it solidifies as many small, independent granules. Layered on top of that, **vacuum-induced freezing** (file 03 §2a) uses the vacuum draw itself to pull additional latent heat out of the product through evaporative cooling as some free water flashes off under reduced pressure — Hosokawa's own description of this effect is that it produces "a granular, snow-like ice structure" specifically *because* it's vacuum-induced rather than purely jacket-conduction-cooled. The combination — cold wall, continuous gentle agitation, and a vacuum-assisted final freeze — is what turns "freezing a liquid in a cone" into "producing a bed of small ice granules with a very favorable surface-area-to-volume ratio for fast, uniform sublimation" (file 02 §5–6 covers why that surface area matters so much for drying speed).

## 4. Thermal duty for the freezing stage — extending file 11's method

File 11 §3 already walks through the sensible-heat-plus-latent-heat-of-fusion calculation for a representative 10 kg batch. The same method extends directly to any other batch size or target freeze time — the governing equation doesn't change:

```
Q_total = m × cp_liquid × ΔT_cool-to-freeze-point  +  m_water × ΔH_fusion  +  m × cp_solid × ΔT_further-cooling
```

Two refinements worth adding once you're sizing a real design rather than a first-pass estimate: (1) add the **vessel wall's own heat capacity** (mass of the wetted metal × its specific heat × the same temperature swing) to the total — for a thick-walled jacketed cone this is not always negligible next to the product load, especially on a small-batch machine where the metal-to-product mass ratio is much less favorable than on a large one; and (2) add an **ambient heat infiltration allowance** (heat leaking in through imperfect insulation and any non-insulated fittings) as a continuous parasitic load on top of the batch's own cooling duty — commonly estimated as a percentage margin (10–20% is a reasonable planning allowance before you have measured data) rather than calculated in detail until you have a real prototype to test against, consistent with file 11's general guidance to prefer measured values over assumed ones wherever you can get them.

## 5. Agitator torque and shear — what actually happens as the batch freezes

File 11 §5 already flags agitator power as something to measure rather than predict precisely. Worth adding here: **torque during the freezing stage is not constant, and its *shape* over time is diagnostic.** As the batch transitions from liquid, to slush, to fully frozen granular solid, drive torque/current (`JIT-101` on the P&ID, file 19) typically rises through the slush phase — the point of highest viscous and frictional resistance — then *falls* again once the product has fully broken into free-flowing granules, since granular solids moving past each other and the wall generally resist motion less than a thick, partially-frozen slush does. A torque curve that rises but never comes back down by the time the product should be fully frozen is a useful, early, real-time signal that something is off (uneven freezing, a colder region caking rather than granulating) well before you'd otherwise detect it — well worth logging and reviewing on every batch during your commissioning and early process-development work (file 15).
